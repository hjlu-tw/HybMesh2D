Abstract

This paper presents an integrated CFD workflow platform designed to reduce the adoption barrier of in-house compressible-flow solvers without modifying the solver core. The platform was developed using a modular, Python-based architecture and unifies geometry preparation, hybrid mesh generation, case configuration, solver execution, convergence monitoring, and result visualization within a single environment, thereby eliminating numerous manual operations traditionally required throughout the simulation process. Development was carried out using an AI-assisted scientific software development approach [2,4,6], employing cloud-based and locally deployed LLM coding assistants in agentic mode — a paradigm informally characterized as responsible vibe coding — in which the domain expert retains full responsibility for workflow design, numerical validation, and system architecture while the AI accelerates implementation. The platform was applied to the UNICONES compressible flow solver [1] and evaluated from a workflow perspective: the integrated environment reduced the number of required software tools from five to one and decreased the number of workflow steps from approximately twenty to five. Based on informal observation, onboarding time for new users completing a standard simulation case was estimated to decrease from approximately two weeks to two days. The methodology suggests a generalizable design strategy for research groups seeking to broaden the adoption of in-house simulation tools while operating with limited software engineering resources.

Keywords: CFD workflow integration; solver accessibility; simulation environment; AI-assisted scientific software development; human–AI collaborative programming; agentic coding; LLM-based software development; scientific software

1. Introduction

CFD solvers developed within research organizations often achieve high levels of numerical maturity, yet their practical adoption remains limited due to the complexity of the surrounding simulation workflow. While commercial CFD packages such as STAR-CCM+ and ANSYS Fluent integrate these steps into a unified environment, open-source and in-house solvers typically lack such interfaces, requiring users to coordinate separate tools, manage manual file conversions, and navigate solver-specific configuration with little to no GUI support. This operational complexity creates a significant barrier for students and new researchers, even when the underlying solver itself is well validated.

This challenge is particularly acute for research groups that develop and maintain in-house solvers. UNICONES [1], a compressible Euler/Navier–Stokes solver based on the Conservation Element and Solution Element (CESE) method [5], developed at the National Center for High-performance Computing (NCHC), Taiwan, is one such example. The solver is intended for broader academic use and is available to interested researchers upon inquiry. In practice, however, the full simulation workflow requires coordinating multiple preprocessing utilities, manual file format conversions, and solver configuration steps that are not yet documented in a user-facing interface. As a result, familiarization typically demands several weeks of guided effort, and hands-on experience has so far remained limited to a small number of interns and collaborators. This gap — between a mature, validated solver with open-access intent and the operational complexity that impedes its adoption — is representative of a broader challenge faced by research groups that develop and maintain in-house CFD solvers.

This work presents an integrated CFD workflow platform designed to reduce the adoption barrier of an in-house compressible-flow solver without modifying the solver core. The platform unifies geometry preparation, mesh generation, case configuration, solver execution, convergence monitoring, and result visualization within a single environment, thereby eliminating numerous manual operations traditionally required throughout the simulation process. The platform is demonstrated with UNICONES, but its modular architecture is designed with extensibility to other solvers in mind. A distinctive aspect of this work is the development methodology: the platform was built through AI-assisted scientific software development [2,4], a human–AI collaborative programming approach in which domain experts retain responsibility for workflow design, numerical validation, and system architecture, while leveraging large language models to accelerate implementation. This paradigm has been informally characterized as responsible vibe coding — a term drawing on the concept popularized by Karpathy [2] but extended here to emphasize disciplined human oversight and physical validation responsibility.

This paper makes the following contributions: (i) the design and implementation of a modular, integrated CFD workflow platform that lowers the adoption barrier of in-house solvers for students and new researchers; (ii) a documented case study of AI-assisted scientific software development applied to CFD tooling, with quantitative observations on development efficiency and human–AI role division; and (iii) a generalizable workflow integration design strategy applicable to research groups maintaining in-house CFD solvers with limited software engineering resources.

2.1 PreProcessor Module

The PreProcessor provides an interactive geometry canvas for loading, viewing, and resampling surface discretizations. It supports multiple point-spacing strategies including uniform, cosine, geometric, and hyperbolic tangent distributions, enabling independent control over leading- and trailing-edge clustering. Automatic sharp-corner detection identifies feature points where the turning angle exceeds a configurable threshold, allowing the geometry to be split into segments with separate resampling parameters. The resampling backend is implemented as a standalone C++ executable, communicating with the GUI via a QThread worker to maintain interface responsiveness.

2.2 Hybrid Mesh Generator

The mesh generation core (C++, ~1,500 lines) produces 2D hybrid meshes comprising structured quadrilateral boundary layers and unstructured triangular far-field elements. Boundary layers are grown outward from the surface using a node-marching algorithm, with dedicated handling for convex and concave corners. A configurable transition region bridges the boundary-layer zone to the far-field, where the Gmsh library [3] generates unstructured triangles via the Frontal-Delaunay algorithm. The module exports both VTK format for standalone visualization and STAR-CD format (.vrt/.cel/.bnd) for solver input. Automatic format conversion between pipeline stages is handled transparently, eliminating a class of manual operations that previously required separate tools.

2.3 Solver Integration and Adapter Interface

The solver integration module orchestrates the full execution chain from within the GUI: mesh format conversion, optional domain decomposition for MPI parallelism, and solver execution in pthread parallel mode. The module automatically constructs the required case directory structure and generates the solver input configuration. During execution, a background worker parses solver output in real time, extracting iteration counts, CFL numbers, and L2 residuals for five conservation variables, displayed on a live convergence monitor.

To support extensibility to other solvers, the integration layer is organized around a Solver Adapter abstraction. Each adapter implements a well-defined interface comprising two components: (1) a format conversion routine that translates the platform's intermediate mesh representation (VTK) into the solver's required input format; and (2) a stdout parser that extracts convergence quantities from the solver's standard output. The remainder of the platform — geometry preprocessing, mesh generation, case directory management, and visualization — operates independently of the solver and requires no modification. Concretely, the adapter interface requires the following methods:

Table 0. Solver Adapter interface definition. Implementing these two components is sufficient to connect a new solver to the platform.

MethodInputOutputDescription
convert_mesh(src, dst) VTK mesh path Solver-format mesh file(s) Converts platform mesh to solver input format (e.g., STAR-CD .vrt/.cel/.bnd for UNICONES)
parse_stdout(line) → dict One line of solver stdout Dict of convergence quantities or None Extracts iteration, CFL, and residual values for live monitoring; returns None for non-data lines

This two-method interface deliberately minimizes the integration burden: a new solver can be connected by implementing fewer than 100 lines of Python, without any knowledge of the GUI or mesh generation internals. The UNICONES adapter serves as the reference implementation and demonstrates handling of multiple output modes (IBM and non-IBM solver variants), which were discovered during live testing to produce different stdout structures.

2.4 Results Visualization

The results module parses Tecplot-format output files and renders cell-centered scalar fields using an embedded matplotlib canvas. It supports smooth contour rendering, velocity streamlines, and vector glyphs. Users can interactively select field variables, adjust colormaps and color scale limits, and toggle mesh wireframe overlays — all without leaving the application.

3. AI-Assisted Development Methodology

The platform was developed using an approach we characterize as responsible vibe coding — a mode of AI-assisted programming where domain experts maintain full architectural control and validation responsibility, while delegating implementation to LLM-based coding assistants. This section describes the workflow, provides concrete examples, and evaluates the development efficiency.

3.1 The Human–AI Collaboration Model

AI-assisted scientific software development [4,6] refers to a human–AI collaborative programming approach in which developers describe implementation intent in natural language and LLM-based coding assistants generate the corresponding code, with humans retaining architectural responsibility and domain validation. In our context, we adopt a disciplined variant informally termed responsible vibe coding — drawing on the concept popularized by Karpathy [2] but emphasizing that the domain expert maintains full architectural control and validation responsibility. Development used a combination of cloud-based and locally deployed LLM coding assistants operating in agentic mode: rather than simple chat-based code generation, the AI could directly read and write project files, execute the codebase, and inspect runtime outputs. This capability proved critical for diagnosing issues that could not be resolved through textual description alone (see Section 3.2, Case A).

The domain expert was responsible for defining the simulation pipeline architecture and data flow, specifying numerical algorithms, designing the format compatibility chain between pipeline components, and validating all outputs against known physical solutions. The AI was responsible for generating GUI boilerplate, implementing file parsers, producing C++ routines from algorithmic descriptions, and wiring the controller–worker–view architecture. Development proceeded iteratively — describe, generate, execute, validate, correct — typically converging within 2–5 rounds per feature.

3.2 Representative Development Cases

Case A: Boundary-layer generation. The expert described the outward-marching algorithm — including normal vector computation, fan-element geometry at convex corners, and the concave-node blending strategy — in natural language, and the AI produced an initial 610-line C++ implementation. This case illustrated three distinct challenges in human–AI collaboration. First, the quality of the AI's output was strongly dependent on the precision of the human's problem description: more carefully worded prompts consistently reduced the number of correction rounds. Second, textual description alone was often insufficient; the AI required access to actual run results to correctly diagnose geometric edge cases, such as self-intersection at sharp trailing edges and incorrect fan-node counts for acute angles. Third, even after the AI confirmed that a fix was correct, discrepancies emerged during real use — highlighting that AI self-verification carries blind spots that only domain-expert validation can catch. These three layers of human oversight — precise description, execution-based diagnosis, and physical validation — proved essential throughout the development process.

Case B: Complete GUI framework. The PyQt6 application comprises 69 Python source files totaling over 18,000 lines, organized in an MVC architecture with 9 controller mixins, 8 data models, 16 view components, and 15 panel widgets. The AI was highly effective at generating GUI scaffolding — widget layouts, stylesheet definitions, signal/slot wiring — which constitutes the majority of GUI code by volume. Human judgment remained essential, however, for decisions that required understanding of the overall user workflow: specifying mode-switching behavior, ensuring interaction consistency across panels, and maintaining a coherent dark-theme aesthetic throughout the application. These are concerns that the AI could implement once specified, but could not independently define.

Case C: Solver pipeline integration. This was the most complex integration task, requiring the worker to manage case directory structures with file renaming and path rewriting, parse real-time solver stdout for convergence monitoring, and handle both IBM (Immersed Boundary Method) and non-IBM solver output formats — which were discovered during live testing to have different stdout structures. The initial implementation plan was written collaboratively with the AI, documenting 8 key design decisions and 9 risk items, then executed phase-by-phase.

The AI-assisted approach compressed what would typically require 3–6 months of conventional development to approximately 4–6 weeks of part-time effort by a single domain expert without dedicated software engineering support. The AI contribution was unevenly distributed across component types: the estimated AI-generated proportion was highest (~80–90%) for GUI boilerplate, file I/O routines, and standard software patterns (MVC wiring, worker threads), and lowest (~40–60%) for domain-specific numerical logic, third-party SDK integration, and cross-component format agreements that required expert design decisions. These observations are consistent with recent systematic analyses of LLM-based software development [4,6], which find that AI assistants are most effective for well-specified, pattern-matching tasks and least effective where domain knowledge is essential to problem formulation.

5. Discussion

The AI-assisted scientific software development approach [2,4,6] proved most effective for developing the peripheral tooling around an existing solver — GUI interfaces, data pipelines, and visualization — while the solver core (UNICONES) remained untouched. This division aligns naturally with the capabilities of current LLMs: they excel at well-established software patterns such as GUI frameworks, file I/O, and standard algorithms, but require substantial human guidance for domain-specific numerical logic. Critically, as demonstrated in Section 3.2, effective collaboration depends not only on the AI's code generation ability, but on three layers of human oversight: the precision of problem description, execution-based diagnosis of edge cases, and physical validation of results. Without domain expertise at each of these layers, AI-assisted development for scientific software carries significant risk of producing plausible but incorrect implementations. We suggest the informal label responsible vibe coding to emphasize these oversight requirements, distinguishing this practice from uncritical acceptance of AI-generated output.

The Solver Adapter design (Section 2.3) is a deliberate architectural choice with extensibility in mind. Adapting the platform to a different solver requires implementing only two interface methods: the mesh format conversion routine and the stdout parser for convergence monitoring. The remaining modules — geometry preprocessing, mesh generation, and visualization — operate independently of the solver and require no modification. In practice, this amounts to fewer than 100 lines of Python for a new adapter, making the integration burden low enough for a domain expert without dedicated software engineering support. This separation of concerns suggests that the platform architecture could serve as a reusable template for other research groups seeking to build front-ends for their own in-house solvers.

Unlike commercial pre/post-processing tools such as Pointwise or ANSYS ICEM, the present platform is intentionally lightweight and tightly coupled to a specific simulation context. This focused scope is precisely what makes the responsible vibe coding approach viable: the development target is well-defined, the acceptance criteria are clear, and the user base has known needs. We suggest that this pattern — AI-assisted scientific software development of solver-specific workflow environments by domain experts without dedicated software engineering resources — is broadly reproducible across the computational science community. The platform is available to interested researchers upon inquiry.

6. Conclusion

We presented an integrated CFD workflow platform for in-house solvers, developed using an AI-assisted scientific software development approach [2,4,6] — informally characterized as responsible vibe coding — and demonstrated with the UNICONES compressible flow solver. Rather than introducing a new numerical method, this work demonstrates that workflow integration can substantially improve the accessibility and usability of existing CFD technologies. The platform unifies geometry preparation, hybrid mesh generation, solver execution with real-time monitoring, and flow-field visualization into a single graphical application, reducing the required tool count from five to one and estimated onboarding time from two weeks to two days.

The modular solver integration architecture suggests a path toward a more general CFD front-end framework applicable across different in-house solvers. Promising directions for future work include extending the platform to 3D mesh generation, incorporating automated parameter optimization for mesh quality, conducting a formal user study with standardized usability metrics, and exploring web-based deployment to further lower the barrier to entry for CFD education and research.
